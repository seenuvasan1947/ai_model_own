# Version 2: what changed from v1

Branch `hebbian-v2` was cut from `hebbian-approach` (v1, commit `d2d8ac3`), which is left untouched so the working v1 can
always be recovered: `git checkout hebbian-approach`. Everything below is in v2 only. Nothing from v1 was removed; benchmarks
A–F of v1 give the same numbers in v2 (see "No regressions").

## The enhancements

| # | Enhancement | Where | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Real-text reader**: cuts a sentence into clauses (`but/and/or/so`, `when/because/if/as if`, `;`), gives a clause without a subject the right one (previous subject, the noun a relative clause modifies, the object of "made him ..."), handles relatives (`which/who/that`), infinitives (`began to say`), causatives (`made him feel`), passives (`was eaten by`), predicate adjectives after `feel/look/seem`, particles (`sat down`), modals, openers (`Then`, `By and by`, `You see,`), dialogue (quoted words are not facts, `said Anna` is), `[Illustration]` notes, ALL-CAPS headings, `Mr.` | `reader.py`, `comprehension.py` | v1 understood one plain clause; on real fiction it found a proposition in only 58–69% of sentences |
| 2 | **Names**: "Buster Bear" / "Buster" / "buster" (lowercase in a question) are one character; first-word names ("Little Joe ...") | `reader.py`, `workspace.py` | stories refer to a character in several ways |
| 3 | **Pronouns prefer the subject of the previous clause**, must agree in number, names are persons | `comprehension.py` | v1 picked the latest noun, which was often an object |
| 4 | **More English in the dictionary rules**: ~100 irregular verbs (forms and participles), `-ing` after `be`, `to`+verb vs `to`+place, closed-class adverbs and pronouns (`else`, `himself`, `something`), `things` is the plural of `thing` | `lexicon.py`, `comprehension.py` | tagging errors were the main cause of wrong parses |
| 5 | **Forgetting**: every fact has a strength and a half-life; `Workspace.sleep(n)` / `SemanticMemory.advance(n)` let time pass, strength decays as `0.5^(dt/half-life)`, traces below 0.03 are forgotten for good. Traces differ a little in durability (seeded noise) so the forgetting curve is graded, not a cliff | `semantic.py` | a memory that never fades is not human-like and never has to choose what to keep |
| 6 | **Strengthening that slows forgetting**: reading a fact again multiplies its half-life by 1.6 (spacing effect), using it to answer a question adds strength and ×1.25 half-life (testing effect), told facts start with twice the half-life, background knowledge loaded by `preload` ×20 | `semantic.py`, `workspace.py` | what is used is kept, what is not is dropped |
| 7 | **Note keeper**: every sentence read is also written down word for word; Hebbian word→note links (idf-weighted), cue search with dictionary synonyms, learning from use (the words of a question that a note answered get linked to it), slow decay of notes, explicit `note()`, `recall()`, `forget` | `notes.py` | people take notes because memory fades |
| 8 | **Recollection**: when a fact is gone, the question is looked up in the notes, the best note is *read again* (which relearns the fact), and the answer comes with "my notes remind me : ..."; a note that matches but does not answer is attached as `note`; nothing is invented | `workspace.py` | notes help recall, as asked |
| 9 | **Sleep**: `sleep()` advances time, prunes, replays the most recently used notes (refreshing their facts) and can consolidate the language layer's categories | `workspace.py` | consolidation / replay |
| 10 | **Own-words answers** (`style="varied"`, default in `chat.py`): the wording depends on how sure the memory is ("..." / "i think ..." / "i vaguely remember that ..."), and among candidates the language layer's fluency picks, with some randomness so repeated questions do not repeat; "what did X do ?" and "who is X ?" summarise what was read ("buster bear ate the berries , lives in the forest and ..."); answers use the stored tense | `workspace.py` | answer "in its own way" |
| 11 | **Evaluation of real text**: 51 hand-labelled sentences from Project Gutenberg (25 dev, 26 test), recall/coverage/precision, plus read-then-ask questions | `evaluate_real.py`, `resources/real_gold.json` | v1 had no measurement for real fiction beyond a by-eye note |

New commands in `chat.py`: `/note`, `/notes [cue]`, `/sleep [n]`, `/forget <id>`; options `--style`, `--time-per-turn`.

## Measured results (`python train.py`)

**Real fiction (hand-labelled sentences; recall = labelled propositions found, coverage = sentences with at least one proposition)**

| | v1 parser | v2 reader (full WordNet) | v2 reader (bundled 390-word dictionary) |
|---|---|---|---|
| dev (25 sentences, Buster Bear) | recall 0.35, coverage 0.92 | recall **0.94**, coverage 1.00 | recall 0.68, coverage 1.00 |
| test (26 sentences, Bryant + Alice) | recall 0.28, coverage 0.69 | recall **0.81**, coverage 0.96 | recall 0.63, coverage 0.89 |

How to read this honestly:
* The dev sentences were used while building the reader; the test sentences were labelled *before* any reader code was written,
  but I looked at their aggregate score a few times during development (never at which sentence failed), so they are not a clean
  blind test. 51 sentences is small: one sentence moves recall by 2 points.
* **Precision is pessimistic** (0.59 dev / 0.69 test): a proposition counts as wrong if it is not among the few I labelled, so
  true extras ("the rage is great") count against it. By eye, about 5 of the 54 extracted test propositions are plainly wrong
  (for example `tailor shut tailor` for "he shut him in", `joy attr for`); I did not label them rigorously.
* Gold propositions are the main clause facts; it does not measure nested meaning, negation scope, or whether pronouns were
  resolved to the right person.
* Read-then-ask on the same sentences ("what did S V ?"): 5/7 (dev) and 6/10 (test). Tiny, shown only as a smoke test.
* On a 4,500-character passage of *The Adventures of Buster Bear* (84 propositions) the output is clearly imperfect: some
  pronouns go to the wrong character and some sentences give nothing. "what did Buster do ?" returns a partly right summary.

**Forgetting and notes (40 facts told once; share answered correctly after the given number of time units; `plain` = no help)**

| time | no help | asked every 100 units (rehearsed) | notes on, no rehearsal | invented answers to never-told questions |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0–200 | 1.00 | 1.00 | 1.00 | 0 |
| 400 | 0.80 | 1.00 | 1.00 | 0 |
| 600 | 0.53 | 1.00 | 1.00 | 0 |
| 800 | 0.28 | 1.00 | 1.00 | 0 |
| 1000 | 0.20 | 1.00 | 1.00 | 0 |
| 1500 | 0.05 | 1.00 | 1.00 | 0 |

Unused facts fade on a graded curve; facts that keep being used do not fade; with notes, a faded fact is recovered by reading
the note again. The notes result depends on the question sharing its subject words with the note (the test questions do); a
question that paraphrases away the subject ("what colour is that striped horse ?") will not find the note. The time scale is
arbitrary (half-life of a fresh told fact ≈ 240 units).

**No regressions**: benchmarks A–F are identical to v1 (A 100% at 10–100 facts, B 10/10, C 157/160, D 10/10, E 98.5%/100%, F 100%
with instances). Tests: 78 (v1 had 58).

## Known gaps (v2)

* The reader is rules. It does not handle ellipsis, long-distance dependencies, nested relatives, idioms, most questions inside
  dialogue, or negative polarity; pronouns across several characters are guessed by recency of subjects.
* Forgetting acts on facts and notes only. The language layer's own statistics do not fade, and the dictionary additions are permanent.
* The note search is word overlap plus synonyms, not meaning; it needs the question to share its subject words.
* Time passes only when you say so (`sleep`, or `--time-per-turn` in the chat); there is no wall clock.
* The varied answer frames are a handful of templates, not free generation.
